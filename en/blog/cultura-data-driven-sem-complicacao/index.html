Data-Driven Culture without the fuss

It is not Power BI, it is not AI, it is not having lots of data. It is turning reality into consequence.

By Gustavo SantosSeptember 23, 20262 min readTranslated from Portuguese.

Every English buzzword runs the same risk sooner or later: becoming a sticker on the meeting room wall.

"Data-driven" did not escape.

The company buys a tool, opens a few dashboards and there it is: someone announces that decisions are now data-driven.

It would be great if culture came with a software license.

Let's start with what it isn't

Data-Driven Culture is not Power BI.

It is not SQL.

It is not artificial intelligence.

It is not having a team called Data & Analytics.

And it is also not storing everything that happens inside the company until nobody knows where anything is anymore.

These things can help. Some help a great deal. But they are means.

The most important question is another:

"What can the organization do, on a recurring basis, with the signals that reality produces?"

Reality gives signals before it gives reports

A customer who abandons a purchase is a signal.

A time slot that starts to go empty is a signal.

A machine that starts stopping more frequently is a signal.

A salesperson who always receives the same objection is a signal.

A search that grows is a signal.

Data is born when we begin to record these signals in a way that can be compared, interpreted and used.

From there, the crossing begins.

Signal from reality → data → information → evidence → interpretation → communication → decision → action → result → learning → value.

The important point is that the arrow needs to keep moving.

If it stops at the report, we have information.

If it stops at the presentation, we have communication.

If it stops at the meeting, we may have intention.

Data-Driven Culture appears when the organization can take evidence all the way to consequence and learn from what happened afterwards.

Technology amplifies. It does not inaugurate.

Artificial intelligence has made this capacity far more accessible.

Today a person can ask questions in natural language, and a system can search for data, execute queries, cross-reference documents and present hypotheses.

This is extraordinary.

But there is still a question that comes before all of this:

What is worth asking?

Technology speeds up the answer. It does not replace the responsibility of formulating the problem.

A simpler definition

If we need to remove all the technical vocabulary from the room, something like this remains:

"Data-Driven Culture is learning to look at what is happening, ask better questions and use the answers to decide better."

The formal definition is more precise. But practice starts there.

Less "I think it's like this".

More "let's find out".
